The Truth About Internet Speed

Why "fast" connections feel slow, and how we measure the difference.

The "Fixed Duration" Standard

Most speed tests work by downloading a file and waiting for it to finish. This method has significant flaws: on slow connections, you wait forever; on ultra-fast connections, the test finishes before the speed has time to stabilize.

SpeedCheck is different. We use a strictly enforced 10-second window for both download and upload measurements. This approach mirrors modern network engineering standards:

  • Consistency: Every test takes exactly the same amount of time, regardless of speed.
  • Stability: 10 seconds is the "sweet spot" to allow TCP protocols to ramp up (TCP Slow Start) and reach maximum throughput without wasting data.
  • Realism: We measure sustained capacity, which is what matters for streaming a 2-hour movie or a long video call.

Why Amsterdam?

You might notice our server is located in Amsterdam, Netherlands. This is a deliberate choice, not a default setting.

If you live outside Europe (for example, in Africa or South America), testing against a server in your own city is often misleading. It tells you that the cable to your house is fine, but it doesn't tell you how well you can access the global internet.

Since a huge portion of the world's digital content (Netflix, Spotify, Zoom servers) is hosted in or routed through major European hubs like Amsterdam, testing against this location reveals your Real-World International Bandwidth. It accounts for the undersea cables and international routing that local tests ignore.

The Units: Mbps vs. MB/s

One of the most common confusions in internet speed is the unit of measurement.

ISPs sell "Megabits" (Mbps).
Downloads show "Megabytes" (MB/s).

There are 8 bits in 1 byte. This means if you pay for a 100 Mbps connection, your maximum real-world download speed is actually 12.5 MB/s.

SpeedCheck measures in Mbps to match your ISP plan, helping you verify if you are getting what you pay for.
