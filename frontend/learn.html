The Truth About Internet Speed

Why "fast" connections feel slow, and how we measure the difference.

Understanding Internet Speed Testing

Internet speed tests have become an essential tool for diagnosing connectivity issues and verifying service quality. However, not all speed tests are created equal. The methodology, server location, and measurement techniques can dramatically affect the results you see—and their relevance to your actual internet experience.

This guide explains how SpeedCheck approaches internet speed measurement, why our methodology differs from conventional tests, and what your results truly mean for real-world usage.

The Fixed Duration Standard

Traditional speed tests operate by downloading or uploading files of predetermined sizes and measuring how long the transfer takes. While straightforward, this approach introduces significant variability and potential inaccuracies:

  • Duration Inconsistency: Slow connections may require several minutes to complete the test, while gigabit connections finish in seconds.
  • TCP Warm-Up Issues: Fast connections may complete the test before TCP congestion control algorithms have time to ramp up to full speed.
  • User Experience Mismatch: Brief tests do not reflect sustained throughput capabilities needed for extended activities like video streaming or large file transfers.

SpeedCheck employs a fixed-duration methodology. Each measurement phase—download and upload—runs for precisely 10 seconds, regardless of connection speed. This approach aligns with modern network engineering best practices and provides several critical advantages:

Benefits of Fixed Duration Testing

  • Temporal Consistency: Every test completes in the same timeframe, enabling fair comparisons across different times and conditions.
  • TCP Optimization: Ten seconds allows sufficient time for TCP Slow Start to complete and reach sustained maximum throughput without unnecessary data consumption.
  • Realistic Capacity Measurement: Sustained performance over 10 seconds better reflects capacity for typical internet activities such as HD video streaming, video conferencing, and content downloads.
  • Reduced Variability: Longer measurement windows smooth out momentary fluctuations and network jitter, providing more stable and reproducible results.

The 10-second window represents an engineering compromise between test accuracy, user convenience, and data efficiency. It is long enough to measure true sustained capacity while remaining short enough to complete quickly and conserve bandwidth.

Why Amsterdam? The Geography of Internet Performance

SpeedCheck deliberately measures your connection speed to a server located in Amsterdam, Netherlands. For users outside Europe, this may initially seem counterintuitive. However, this geographic choice reveals critical information that local speed tests systematically obscure.

The Limitations of Local Testing

When you test your internet speed against a server in your own city or country, you are primarily measuring the quality of your last-mile connection—the cable from your ISP's local exchange to your home or office. While this information has value, it provides an incomplete picture of your internet experience.

Most internet content and services are not hosted locally. When you stream content from Netflix, join a Zoom call, access cloud services, or download software updates, your traffic typically traverses international networks, passes through submarine cables, and routes through major internet exchange points far from your location.

Amsterdam as a Global Internet Hub

Amsterdam serves as one of Europe's—and by extension, the world's—most significant internet exchange points. The Amsterdam Internet Exchange (AMS-IX) is among the largest internet exchanges globally, handling massive volumes of international traffic. Major content delivery networks, cloud providers, and digital services maintain infrastructure in Amsterdam due to its strategic position in global internet architecture.

Real-World International Bandwidth

Testing against Amsterdam reveals your real-world international bandwidth—the speed at which you can actually access global internet content. This measurement accounts for:

  • International routing efficiency
  • Submarine cable capacity and latency
  • Peering relationships between networks
  • Transit provider performance
  • Geographic distance and propagation delay

For users in regions with developing internet infrastructure—including parts of Africa, South America, and Southeast Asia—the difference between local and international speeds can be dramatic. An ISP might deliver excellent speeds to domestic servers while international connectivity remains constrained by limited undersea cable capacity or inefficient routing.

By testing against Amsterdam, SpeedCheck provides a realistic assessment of your internet's capability to access the global digital ecosystem, not just local resources.

Understanding Latency: The Hidden Performance Factor

While bandwidth (measured in Mbps) receives the most attention, latency—the time required for data to travel from your device to the server and back—fundamentally affects your internet experience, particularly for interactive applications.

What Latency Measures

Latency, measured in milliseconds (ms), represents the round-trip time for data packets. SpeedCheck measures latency through ICMP ping tests, providing insight into the responsiveness of your connection. Low latency is essential for:

  • Video Conferencing: High latency creates awkward delays in conversation and disrupts natural communication flow.
  • Online Gaming: Multiplayer games require low latency for responsive controls and synchronized gameplay.
  • Remote Desktop: Interactive remote computing becomes frustrating with noticeable input lag.
  • VoIP Calling: Voice communication suffers from echo and conversation overlap with elevated latency.

Latency Guidelines

  • Excellent (0-50ms): Ideal for all applications including competitive gaming and real-time collaboration.
  • Good (50-100ms): Suitable for most uses; minor delays may be perceptible in demanding applications.
  • Fair (100-200ms): Adequate for browsing and streaming; noticeable lag in interactive applications.
  • Poor (200ms+): Significant delays affect video calls and make real-time interaction difficult.

Geographic distance fundamentally constrains latency due to the finite speed of light. Data traveling from Africa to Amsterdam must traverse thousands of kilometers of fiber optic cable, imposing a physical minimum latency based on propagation delay. This reality means international latency will inherently exceed local latency, regardless of connection quality.

Bandwidth Units: Mbps vs MB/s

One of the most persistent sources of confusion in internet speed measurement stems from the units used to express data rates. Understanding the distinction between bits and bytes is essential for interpreting speed test results and comparing them to real-world transfer speeds.

The Bit vs Byte Distinction

Digital information is fundamentally measured in bits—the smallest unit of data, representing a single binary value (0 or 1). However, computers organize data into bytes, where one byte equals eight bits. This 8:1 ratio creates the difference between connection speeds and file transfer rates.

Unit Conversion

Internet Service Providers advertise speeds in Megabits per second (Mbps).
File downloads display progress in Megabytes per second (MB/s).

To convert between units: MB/s = Mbps ÷ 8

Practical Examples

  • 100 Mbps connection → Maximum download speed of 12.5 MB/s
  • 500 Mbps connection → Maximum download speed of 62.5 MB/s
  • 1 Gbps (1000 Mbps) connection → Maximum download speed of 125 MB/s

SpeedCheck reports results in Mbps to align with ISP marketing and plan specifications, enabling direct comparison between advertised speeds and measured performance. When evaluating whether you receive the service you pay for, compare the test results directly to your plan's advertised speed.

Why ISPs Use Mbps

The telecommunications industry standardized on bits per second for historical and technical reasons. Network engineering focuses on bit rates because network equipment operates at the bit level, transmitting individual bits across physical media. Additionally, using Mbps produces larger numbers than MB/s—a factor that may influence marketing considerations.

Factors Affecting Your Speed Test Results

Internet speed measurements reflect the performance of a complex system involving multiple components, any of which may introduce bottlenecks or variability. Understanding these factors helps interpret test results and diagnose connectivity issues.

Network Factors

  • ISP Capacity: Your service plan's bandwidth limit represents the maximum possible speed.
  • Network Congestion: Shared infrastructure becomes slower during peak usage periods when many subscribers are active simultaneously.
  • Routing Efficiency: The path your data takes through the internet affects both speed and latency.
  • Peering Agreements: The quality of connections between your ISP and other networks impacts international speeds.
  • Last-Mile Technology: Fiber connections generally outperform DSL, cable, or wireless technologies.

Local Factors

  • WiFi vs Ethernet: Wireless connections introduce overhead and interference; wired connections provide more consistent performance.
  • Device Capabilities: Older computers or smartphones may lack the processing power to achieve maximum speeds.
  • Background Activity: Other devices or applications consuming bandwidth reduce available capacity for the speed test.
  • Router Performance: Consumer-grade routers may struggle with very high speeds or many simultaneous connections.
  • Browser Limitations: Browser configuration, extensions, or resource constraints can affect test accuracy.

Best Practices for Accurate Testing

  • Use a wired Ethernet connection when possible
  • Close unnecessary applications and browser tabs
  • Pause downloads, streaming, and cloud backups
  • Ensure no other devices are actively using the connection
  • Test at different times of day to identify congestion patterns
  • Run multiple tests and consider the average result

Interpreting Your Results

Speed test results provide valuable diagnostic information, but require context for proper interpretation. A single test represents a snapshot of performance at a specific moment under specific conditions.

Download Speed

Download bandwidth determines how quickly you can retrieve data from the internet. This metric directly affects:

  • Video streaming quality (HD requires 5-8 Mbps, 4K requires 25+ Mbps)
  • File download times
  • Web page loading speed
  • Software update installation

Upload Speed

Upload bandwidth controls how quickly you can send data to the internet. Upload speed is particularly important for:

  • Video conferencing and livestreaming
  • Cloud backup and file synchronization
  • Uploading photos and videos to social media
  • Remote work applications and VPN connections

Most residential internet connections are asymmetric, providing significantly higher download than upload speeds. This design reflects typical consumer usage patterns where downloading content is more common than uploading.

Comparing Results to Your Plan

ISP advertised speeds typically represent maximum theoretical capacity under ideal conditions. In practice, achieving 80-95% of advertised speed is reasonable, while consistently receiving below 70% may indicate an issue warranting investigation.

Remember that testing to Amsterdam measures international performance. If you consistently achieve excellent speeds to local servers but poor speeds internationally, the limitation likely lies in your ISP's international connectivity rather than your local connection.

The Future of Speed Testing

As internet infrastructure continues to evolve, speed testing methodologies must adapt to remain relevant. Several trends are shaping the future of connection measurement:

Multi-Server Testing

Testing against multiple geographic locations simultaneously can reveal routing inefficiencies and provide a more comprehensive view of global connectivity. Future iterations of SpeedCheck may incorporate distributed testing to measure performance to various regions.

Quality of Experience Metrics

Beyond raw bandwidth and latency, newer metrics attempt to quantify actual user experience. Measurements of packet loss, jitter (latency variation), and bufferbloat provide insight into connection quality that simple speed numbers miss.

Application-Specific Testing

Different applications have different requirements. Streaming video demands consistent bandwidth, while video conferencing requires low latency and minimal jitter. Future speed tests may simulate specific application workloads to provide more relevant performance indicators.

Conclusion

Internet speed testing serves as both a diagnostic tool and a means of accountability, enabling users to verify service quality and identify connectivity issues. SpeedCheck's fixed-duration methodology and Amsterdam server location provide measurement data that reflects real-world international internet performance rather than idealized local conditions.

Understanding the technical factors that influence speed test results—from TCP congestion control to international routing to the bit-byte distinction—empowers you to interpret measurements accurately and make informed decisions about your internet service.

While speed tests cannot solve connectivity problems directly, they provide the empirical data necessary to diagnose issues, evaluate service providers, and understand the capabilities and limitations of your internet connection in an increasingly connected world.
